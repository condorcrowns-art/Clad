Ten Spanish sounds an English mouth gets wrong

Not a list of what the letters say — a list of what your mouth does instead, and what to do about it.

Pronunciation advice usually stops at "the Spanish r is rolled", which tells you the target and nothing about how to hit it. The useful part is the comparison: what your tongue is doing now, what it needs to do instead, and which English sound is already close enough to build from.

Each one below is rated by how much it costs you. High means it changes words — perro and pero are dog and but — so getting it wrong is not an accent, it is a different sentence.

Practise these out loud →

The rolled rhigh cost

Written: rr, and r at the start of a word

What your mouth does instead. English has no trill, so you reach for the American r, which is made with the tongue bunched at the back and never touching anything. It is about as far from a Spanish rr as a sound can be.

What it should do. Put your tongue tip just behind your top teeth, where you say "d". Now push air past it and let it flutter — you are not moving the tongue, the air is moving it. Relax the tongue; a tense tongue cannot vibrate.

The shortcut. Say "pot of tea" fast, over and over, in an American accent. The "t of t" is already a tap. Speed it up and lean on it and it becomes a trill.

Words to practise: rojo, ropa, río, rápido, perro, carro, guitarra, arriba, ferrocarril.

The single tap rhigh cost

Written: r between vowels, and at the end

What your mouth does instead. English speakers either substitute the American r — which is a different sound entirely — or over-roll it into a trill, which turns "caro" into "carro" and changes the word.

What it should do. One flick. The tongue tip touches the ridge behind your teeth once and leaves. It is over before you notice it.

The shortcut. It is the same sound as the tt in "butter" or the dd in "ladder" in an American accent. You already make it a hundred times a day.

Words to practise: para, pero, caro, hora, mira, quiero, comer, hablar, trabajar.

The jhigh cost

Written: j always, and g before e or i

What your mouth does instead. English speakers use their h, which is only breath with no friction. "Jamón" comes out as "hamón", which is understandable but immediately marks you as a beginner.

What it should do. Back of the tongue near the roof of the mouth, and scrape. It is the ch in Scottish "loch" or German "Bach" — friction, not just breath.

The shortcut. Start to say "k" and then let the air keep flowing instead of releasing it cleanly. That leak is the sound.

Words to practise: jamón, hijo, trabajo, mujer, ojo, gente, girar, general, ejercicio.

The ñmedium cost

Written: ñ

What your mouth does instead. English speakers say n + y, as in "canyon". Close, and understandable, but it is two sounds where Spanish has one, and it is audible.

What it should do. The whole front of your tongue flat against the roof of the mouth, and the sound comes down your nose. One sound, not two.

The shortcut. Say "onion" and freeze on the middle. That is nearly it — now make it a single movement rather than a slide.

Words to practise: año, niño, España, mañana, señor, pequeño, compañero, cumpleaños.

b and v are the samemedium cost

Written: b and v, always

What your mouth does instead. English speakers make a real v, with the top teeth on the bottom lip. That sound does not exist in Spanish at all. And they make a hard b between vowels, where a Spaniard softens it.

What it should do. At the start of a word or after m or n: both lips, a normal b. Between vowels: bring the lips close but do not close them — the air keeps flowing, like a b that never quite commits.

The shortcut. There is nothing to learn about telling b from v, because they are the same sound. "Vaca" and "baca" are homophones. What is worth learning is the soft version between vowels.

Words to practise: vaca, vino, bueno, saber, trabajo, nuevo, llevar, escribir.

The soft dmedium cost

Written: d between vowels, and at the end of a word

What your mouth does instead. English d is made further back, on the ridge, and is fully stopped. Used between vowels it makes "nada" sound clipped and foreign.

What it should do. Tongue tip lightly on the *back of the top teeth* — further forward than English — and let air escape. It is the th in "this", not the d in "dog".

The shortcut. Say "nada" as "na-tha", with the th of "father". That is much closer than anything with an English d in it.

Words to practise: nada, cada, todo, dedo, ciudad, usted, verdad, estudiar.

ll and ylow cost

Written: ll, and y before a vowel

What your mouth does instead. English speakers say an l, or the j of "jam". Neither is wrong enough to stop you being understood, which is why it survives for years.

What it should do. The middle of the tongue rises to the roof of the mouth. In most of the Spanish-speaking world this is the y in "yes"; in Argentina and Uruguay it is the s in "measure".

The shortcut. Start from the y in "yes" and you are already right almost everywhere.

Words to practise: llave, calle, pollo, ella, yo, ya, ayer, llamar, lluvia.

Five pure vowelshigh cost

Written: every vowel, every time

What your mouth does instead. English vowels glide — "no" is really "no-oo", "say" is "se-ee". Bring that into Spanish and every word wobbles. English also reduces unstressed vowels to "uh", which Spanish never does: the o in "teléfono" is a full o.

What it should do. Each one is a single steady sound that does not move while you say it. a as in "father", e as in "bet", i as in "machine", o as in "or", u as in "food". Say them short and do not let them drift.

The shortcut. Hold each vowel for two seconds and listen for movement. If the sound changes while you hold it, it is an English vowel.

Words to practise: casa, mesa, piso, todo, mucho, teléfono, universidad, oportunidad.

h is silentlow cost

Written: h, everywhere

What your mouth does instead. Every English speaker breathes on it once. "Hola" is "ola", exactly the same as the word for wave.

What it should do. Nothing at all. Say the word as though the letter were not printed. Not a soft h, not a slight breath — no sound whatsoever.

The shortcut. There is no trick. It is silent in every word, with no exceptions.

Words to practise: hola, hasta, hombre, hora, hacer, hijo, ahora, hospital.

Where the stress fallshigh cost

Written: every word of more than one syllable

What your mouth does instead. English speakers carry over the English stress pattern, which puts it near the front. "Teléfono" becomes "TE-le-fo-no" and stops being a word.

What it should do. Push harder on one syllable. Spanish stress is a rule, not a memory: an accent mark decides it; otherwise a word ending in a vowel, n or s is stressed on the second-last syllable, and anything else on the last.

The shortcut. Stress changes meaning: hablo is "I speak", habló is "he spoke". Getting it wrong can change the tense of what you said.

Words to practise: teléfono, música, difícil, español, hablar, trabajo, universidad.

Hearing it is the hard part

You cannot reliably make a distinction you cannot hear, and the pairs above are the test. Lunosia plays them, listens to you say them back, and tells you which sound broke rather than only that the word was wrong — because the second is something you already knew.
